When a star like the Sun runs out of fuel, its core shrinks into a white dwarf: a Sun's worth of mass in a ball the size of the Earth, held up by nothing but the refusal of electrons to share a quantum state. In 1930 a 19-year-old Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar showed that this support has a ceiling. Add mass, and the electrons are squeezed until they move near the speed of light; then their pressure can no longer keep up with gravity, and no size is stable. This page builds that argument one step at a time, and solves the equations live in your browser.

01

A star is a balance

Every layer of a star carries the weight of all the layers above it. It can do that only if the pressure below it is higher than the pressure above it.

Cut a thin shell out of a star at radius r. Gravity pulls it in with the mass m(r) inside it; the pressure is larger on its inner face than on its outer face, and that difference pushes it out. If the two do not cancel, the shell moves, and in a star that happens on a time scale of minutes. Stars last billions of years, so they are in hydrostatic equilibrium:

Integrate it once in rough form and you get the pressure a star of mass M and radius R needs at its centre: Pc ~ G M² / R⁴. Squeeze the star and the needed pressure rises steeply.

In the Sun that pressure is thermal: hot gas at 15 million kelvin, kept hot by hydrogen fusion. Thermal pressure has a weakness: it lasts only as long as something replaces the heat that leaks out. When the fuel runs out, the core loses heat, it shrinks, and by the virial theorem half the gravitational energy it releases heats it again. The core of a star like the Sun goes through helium burning to carbon and oxygen, and then it is too light to ever get hot enough to burn carbon.

The outer layers drift away as a planetary nebula, lit up for a few ten-thousand years by the ultraviolet light of the bare core. That core is a white dwarf: about 0.6 solar masses in a ball the size of the Earth, with no fuel at all. It will cool for the rest of time and it will not shrink. Something other than heat holds it up.

02

Pauli's rule makes pressure

No two electrons may share one quantum state. Pack them into a small box, and most of them are forced to move fast, even at zero temperature.

An electron in a box of side L has a wave that must fit the box, so its momentum comes in steps of 2πħ/L along each axis. Each allowed momentum is one cell of momentum space, and each cell takes two electrons, one spin up and one spin down. That is the Pauli exclusion principle (1925), and electrons, as fermions, obey it without exception.

Fill the box with N electrons at zero temperature. They take the lowest cells first, so they fill a ball in momentum space up to the Fermi momentum pF. Count the cells: the ball's volume over the cell volume, times two.

So pF ∝ n1/3: halve the box side and every electron's top momentum doubles. Electrons moving in a box push on its walls: that is degeneracy pressure. It needs no heat, so it does not drain away as the star cools. The same result follows from Heisenberg's Δx Δp ≳ ħ: an electron confined to a cell of size n−1/3 must carry a momentum of order ħ n1/3.

When does the zero-temperature picture hold? When the Fermi energy is far above kT. At the mean density of Sirius B, EF ≈ 260 keV; its core temperature of order 10⁷ K gives kT ≈ 1 keV. The electrons are about as cold, in this sense, as electrons can be.

03

Slow electrons: a star that shrinks as it grows

While the electrons are slow, degeneracy pressure is stiff enough to stop any mass. But the more mass, the smaller the star.

Pressure is momentum flux: roughly n × p × v. For slow electrons v = p / me, so P ~ n pF² / me, and with pF ∝ n1/3:

The electrons set the pressure but the ions set the weight: each electron comes with μe mu of mass, where μe = 2 for helium, carbon and oxygen. So ρ = μe mu ne. A gas with P = K ρ1+1/n is a polytrope of index n; here n = 1.5.

Now balance it against gravity. With ρ ~ M / R³, the pressure scales as M5/3 / R⁵, and gravity needs G M² / R⁴:

There is always a solution. A heavier star is smaller and denser, with the electrons pushed to higher momentum; but some radius always balances it. With this law alone there is no limit. That is what Ralph Fowler found in 1926, the year Fermi-Dirac statistics was born.

04

Fast electrons: the key step

Squeeze hard enough and pF passes mec. The electrons cannot go faster than light, so their pressure grows more slowly with density, just when gravity needs it most.

An electron's energy is √(p²c² + me²c⁴). For p ≪ mec the kinetic part is p²/2me; for p ≫ mec it is pc. The speed saturates at c, so the momentum flux becomes n × p × c, not n × p²/m:

The exponent falls from 5/3 to 4/3. That looks small. It is everything, and the cleanest way to see why is the energy argument that Landau gave in 1932.

Take N electrons in a ball of radius R. Each has momentum about pF ~ ħ N1/3/R. Relativistic, each carries energy pFc, so the total electron energy is N4/3ħc/R. Gravity gives −G M²/R. Both scale as 1/R:

If the bracket is positive, E rises toward R = 0, so the star does not shrink that far. At large R the electrons slow down, their energy falls off as 1/R², faster than gravity's 1/R, and E turns negative. In between lies a minimum: a stable white dwarf. If the bracket is negative, E falls without bound as R → 0: shrinking always releases energy, and nothing stops it. The sign flips at one mass, because N² grows faster than N4/3:

The radius has dropped out. The limit is built from ħ (quantum), c (relativity), G (gravity) and the proton mass, and nothing else. It is a Planck mass cubed over a proton mass squared: about 1.8 M☉ for a uniform ball, 1.46 M☉ for the real profile.

05

The exact answer

Scaling gives the form of the limit. To get the number, solve the star: hydrostatic balance plus the equation of state, from the centre to the surface.

For a polytrope, put ρ = ρcθⁿ and r = aξ into the balance equation. Everything about the star folds into one equation, published by Lane (1870) and Emden (1907):

Its first zero ξ₁ is the surface, and ωn = −ξ₁²θ′(ξ₁) sets the mass. For n = 3, the relativistic gas, the central density cancels out of the mass: every n = 3 white dwarf has the same mass, whatever its size. That mass is the limit:

With the solver's ω₃ = 2.018236 and μe = 2, it gives 1.456 M☉. Chandrasekhar wrote the formula with the hydrogen mass mH in place of mu, and quoted 5.76/μe² with the constants of his day; that is the textbook 1.44. The mass per electron of carbon-12 is exactly 2 mu, so mu is the right choice for a carbon-oxygen dwarf.

06

Past the limit

A core that crosses the line has three ways out: it burns, it turns into neutrons, or nothing at all stops it.

Electron capture. As the core shrinks the Fermi energy climbs to several MeV. Then it pays for an electron to vanish into a proton, p + e⁻ → n + ν. Each capture removes an electron and its pressure, the core shrinks faster, captures speed up: a runaway. Near 10¹⁰ g/cm³ that happens in an oxygen-neon core; in the iron core of a massive star it happens when the core grows to its own Chandrasekhar mass.

Neutron stars. The collapse stops only when the core reaches nuclear density, 2.8 × 10¹⁴ g/cm³, about 1.4 M☉ in a ball 12 km in radius. Neutrons are fermions too, and their degeneracy pressure plus the repulsion of the strong force hold it up. Neutrons hit the same wall at a higher mass: the Tolman-Oppenheimer-Volkoff limit, about 2.2 to 2.3 M☉ in current estimates. The heaviest pulsar weighed well, PSR J0740+6620, has 2.08 ± 0.07 M☉. The Crab Nebula below holds one, spinning 30 times a second.

Black holes. Above the TOV limit no known pressure is enough. In general relativity pressure itself has weight, so pushing harder only adds to gravity. The core falls inside its Schwarzschild radius rs = 2GM/c², 7 km for 2.5 M☉, and becomes a black hole.

Type Ia supernovae and the accelerating universe

2011 Nobel Prize

A carbon-oxygen white dwarf that gains mass from a companion, as in the render at the top, compresses and heats as it nears MCh. Carbon ignites in its centre; in degenerate matter the pressure does not rise with temperature, so the star cannot expand to cool itself, and the burning runs away. In about a second the whole star burns, much of it to nickel-56, and it is torn apart. The radioactive decay of that nickel powers the light for weeks.

Because these explosions happen in similar stars near a similar mass, their peak brightness is similar. After a correction for the width of the light curve (Phillips 1993) they are standard candles, visible across billions of light years. In 1998 two teams found that distant Type Ia supernovae are fainter than a decelerating universe allows: the expansion is speeding up. Saul Perlmutter, Brian Schmidt and Adam Riess shared the 2011 Nobel Prize in Physics for it.

An honest note: many Type Ia supernovae may come from white dwarfs below MCh (double detonations) or from merging pairs. The calibration of the candle is empirical, so the cosmology does not depend on which path is right, but "they all explode at exactly 1.4" is too simple.

The voyage, and the argument with Eddington

history

In July 1930 Chandrasekhar, nineteen, sailed from Bombay to Venice on his way to study at Cambridge with Ralph Fowler. Fowler had shown in 1926 that degeneracy pressure holds up white dwarfs. On the ship Chandrasekhar asked how fast the electrons in Sirius B move, found speeds close to light, and put special relativity into Fowler's theory. The pressure law turned to n4/3, and a maximum mass appeared. Wilhelm Anderson (1929) and Edmund Stoner (1930) had found limits for uniform stars; Chandrasekhar's 1931 paper, The maximum mass of ideal white dwarfs, gave it with the polytrope, and his 1935 papers gave the full curve on this page. Lev Landau reached the energy argument independently in 1932.

On 11 January 1935, at the Royal Astronomical Society, Arthur Eddington, the most famous astronomer of the day, rose after Chandrasekhar's talk and ridiculed the result: "I think there should be a law of Nature to prevent a star from behaving in this absurd way!" Eddington argued that relativistic degeneracy was a misuse of physics. Most physicists privately sided with Chandrasekhar, but few said so in public, and the limit was slow to be accepted. Chandrasekhar shared the 1983 Nobel Prize in Physics, with William Fowler, for his work on the structure and evolution of stars.

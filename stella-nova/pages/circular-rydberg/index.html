Circular Rydberg atoms: giant orbits that refuse to decay

Push the outer electron of an atom far enough out, and give it all the angular momentum it can hold, and quantum mechanics gives back something close to Bohr's old picture: an electron on a circle. At the University of Stuttgart, Einius Pultinevicius, Florian Meinert and colleagues made such circular orbits in single strontium atoms up to principal quantum number n = 103, a ring 1.1 µm across, about ten thousand times wider than the atom it came from. Between two transparent mirrors at room temperature these atoms kept their state for more than 10 milliseconds, the longest Rydberg lifetimes measured in a laboratory so far.

01

The orbital: a ring instead of a cloud

An electron in an atom is a probability cloud. Its shape is set by three numbers: n (the shell), l (the angular momentum) and m (how that angular momentum points). For most states the cloud is spread out, with nodes and lobes. When l and m take their largest values, l = |m| = n − 1, almost all of that structure goes away. What is left is a thin torus around the nucleus: a circular Rydberg state, written |nC⟩.

02

Everything scales with n

Rydberg atoms are extreme because their properties grow or shrink as powers of n. At n ≈ 100 the powers make them giants: a millionth of a metre across, sensitive to the smallest field, and, for circular states, nearly unable to radiate.

03

Why a circular state lasts: one way down, and a warm sky

An excited atom loses energy by emitting a photon, and each photon can change l by exactly one. A circular state has l = n − 1, and the only state below it with l = n − 2 is the next circular state, |n−1 C⟩. So instead of dropping straight to the ground state with a high-energy optical photon, the electron can only step down one rung at a time with a weak microwave photon. Its natural lifetime grows as n⁵: about 1 second at n = 101.

At room temperature every microwave mode near 10 GHz holds hundreds of thermal photons. They drive the atom up and down the ladder hundreds of times faster than it decays on its own. The plates of the experiment, 10.5 mm apart, remove the modes that carry circularly polarized (σ) light below c/2d.

The rate, term by term

Each final state s adds its Einstein coefficient A, multiplied by the number of thermal photons n̄ at that frequency. The one spontaneous channel, down to |n−1 C⟩, adds the extra 1. The Purcell factor ξ says how much the surroundings change the density of photon modes: 1 in free space, near 0 for σ light between the plates below the cut-off.

At n = 101 and 300 K, n̄ is about 1000, so blackbody light, not spontaneous decay, sets the lifetime: our hydrogen model gives about 0.5 ms in free space, against 0.55 ms in the paper's calculation, and about 1 s at 0 K.

04

Making one: from a cloud to a ring, then up the ladder

A laser cannot make a circular state directly: light carries too little angular momentum. The team first holds one strontium-88 atom in an optical tweezer, excites one electron with lasers to a Rydberg state of low l, and then turns it into |79C⟩ with a circularly polarized radio-frequency field, a method described in their 2024 work. From there they climb: two microwave photons at a time, n → n + 2, each step a Landau-Zener sweep of the electric field across the two-photon resonance.

05

Staying stable: two mirrors that see through

Earlier experiments with long-lived circular atoms cooled the whole apparatus to a few kelvin, so that there were few thermal photons left. This team kept the room temperature and changed the photons instead. The atom sits midway between two glass plates coated with indium tin oxide: transparent for the lasers, but about 96 % reflective for microwaves. Between such plates, a mode of σ light whose wavelength is longer than twice the gap cannot exist, so the atom cannot absorb or emit it. This is the inhibited emission that Kleppner described in 1981, now used to protect a quantum state.

Where the population goes

our rate model

Blackbody photons move the atom one rung up or down; from there it can come back. That is why the paper fits a rate model to all the n manifolds, not one exponential to the first state. Our model has the circular ladder and one bin for everything else (elliptical states).

What the measurements show

Pultinevicius et al. 2026
  • Lifetimes rise sharply near n ≈ 80, where the wavelength of the n → n ± 1 photons passes twice the plate gap: about 5 ms around n = 85.
  • A dip near n ≈ 90 comes from a π-polarized mode of the cylinder formed by the plates and the ring electrodes. Ideal plates do not have it, so our dashed line misses it too.
  • Above n ≈ 95 a second step appears, when the weaker n → n + 2 photons are also cut off: 11.5(8) ms at n = 101, about 21 times the free-space value of 545 µs.
  • That is the lifetime a free atom would have in a 14 K environment, and three times longer than the best cryogenic result so far.
  • The strontium ion core is polarizable, so an ordinary Gaussian tweezer still holds the Rydberg atom: 133(6) ms 1/e trap lifetime from |97C⟩. With the tweezer off, the signal is gone in about 1 ms.
  • Each lifetime point comes from 4000 repetitions per hold time, read out by state-selective field ionization.
06

How big is n = 103?

The mean radius of a circular state is n(n + ½) Bohr radii. At n = 103 that is about 0.56 µm, an orbit 1.1 µm across: as wide as a bacterium, from a single electron bound to a single ion.

07

What it is for

Arrays of neutral atoms excited to Rydberg states are one of the main platforms for quantum simulation and quantum computing. The usual Rydberg states (S and D orbitals) last about 100 µs, which limits how long a simulation can run and how good a gate can be.

Longer quantum simulations

With lifetimes above 10 ms, the fundamental T₁ limit moves about two orders of magnitude beyond today's Rydberg simulators. The authors say the next step is coherence: technical magnetic and electric field noise now limits T₂ to about 100 µs.

Stronger interactions

Interactions grow fast with n. Compared with circular atoms at n ≈ 50, n ≈ 100 gives a van der Waals blockade about a thousand times stronger and a dipole exchange coupling more than ten times larger.

Sensing and new encodings

A ladder of coherently linked circular states can act as a qudit or a synthetic dimension. The inner electron of strontium also allows optical addressing, cooling and clock spectroscopy of the ion core inside the Rydberg orbit.

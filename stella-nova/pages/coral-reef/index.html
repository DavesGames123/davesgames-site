Deep Ocean Explorer

A squad of ROVs explores an endless seafloor in the dark. The corals grew once, long ago, by diffusion-limited aggregation.

Coral growth: a port of dlaf by Michael Fogleman (MIT). Full credits in How it works.

How it works

An endless seafloor

The seafloor has no edge. One seeded field makes it everywhere: shelves, slopes and plains with sand in the low ground, seamounts and flat-topped guyots, ridges, terraced plateaus and drop-off cliffs. Canyons cut into the slopes with vertical, undercut walls and a sand floor. Natural arches, pinnacles with hoodoo caps, boulders and scree sit on it, and the rock has strata ledges and craggy joints. The same seed always gives the same world.

The page keeps only the part near the camera, in chunks of 32 m. Workers mesh each chunk with surface nets on one global lattice, so two chunks meet with the same vertices and no seam. The rock near you is finest; far chunks are coarser and go into the black water.

From the shallows to the trenches

A slower field sets the depth of each region: the sunlit shallows (a few metres, sand flats and bright turquoise water with strong caustics), the reef (5 to 40 m, the densest corals), the reef wall that drops into the twilight zone (40 to 1000 m, fading blue, plate corals, then large gorgonian fans, Lophelia thickets, black and bamboo corals and sponges), and the abyss (3000 m and more) with trenches near 5000 m, where only the ROV lamps light the water. The light, the fog, the caustics and the marine snow follow the depth of the camera. Below about 40 m the corals take the pigments of deep corals: bubblegum pink, orange, gold and white.

Why coral looks like DLA

Many corals feed on food that drifts in the water. Near the colony the water is slow, so the food reaches the coral mostly by diffusion, a random walk. A random walker that comes near a branched shape almost always touches a tip first: the gaps between branches are screened. So tips catch more food, grow faster, and stay ahead. That is diffusion-limited aggregation (DLA), and it is why a nutrient-limited coral grows branches (Kaandorp, Lowe, Frenkel and Sloot, Phys. Rev. Lett. 77, 2328, 1996; Kaandorp and Kübler, The Algorithmic Beauty of Seaweeds, Sponges and Corals, 2001).

The real dlaf walk of one particle. Far from the cluster it jumps by its distance to the cluster, then it steps until it is near enough to join. It nearly always joins a tip.

Corals that grew long ago

Each chunk gets its colonies from its habitat: more on the shallow seamount tops and ridges, few on the deep sand plains. Sites are exposed rock; the species comes from the site (tables and staghorn on open tops, sea fans on walls across the current, brain and finger corals in shelter). A worker grows every colony of the chunk to its end with the shared DLA model, so neighbours compete and fuse, then meshes them as signed distance surfaces. The chunk shows its corals only when all of them are done: they do not grow in front of you, they are just there.

The ROV squad

Two to four hybrid ROVs explore for ever. The lead picks unexplored ground, follows canyons and ridges, passes under arches and stops to circle big colonies; the others keep a loose formation, or follow its trail where the water is narrow. Every path keeps clear of the rock and the coral. They carry no surface cable: a hair-thin fibre-optic micro-tether does not show on camera, and the low data rate of the link is why the camera feed comes as slow-scan pictures (the SSTV encoder and decoder of this site: Robot 36 or Martin M1).

Colour by

Age is the order of joining in each chunk. Depth counts the hops from the seed. Height is over the colony base. Flow is the size of the subtree that a polyp feeds. Light is an estimate from the height and the number of close neighbours.

Coral reef growth

An ROV dive in dark water. Marine snow falls and drifts with the current. A flake that touches a coral sticks: the tips meet the snow first, so the tips grow. Neighbour colonies compete for the same snow and fuse where they meet. You can swim through the reef yourself; the ROV camera comes up as live video or over a slow-scan TV link.

How it works

Why coral looks like DLA

Many corals feed on food that drifts in the water. Near the colony the water is slow, so the food reaches the coral mostly by diffusion, a random walk. A random walker that comes near a branched shape almost always touches a tip first: the gaps between branches are screened. So tips catch more food, grow faster, and stay ahead. That is diffusion-limited aggregation (DLA), and it is why a nutrient-limited coral grows branches.

A current changes the picture. The snow comes from one side, so the side that faces the current gets more and the colony grows upstream. Calm, food-rich water gives compact forms; thin food gives thin branches. Kaandorp and co-workers showed this with DLA in a flow (Kaandorp, Lowe, Frenkel and Sloot, Phys. Rev. Lett. 77, 2328, 1996), and grew whole colonies with an accretive model (Kaandorp and Kübler, The Algorithmic Beauty of Seaweeds, Sponges and Corals, 2001).

The real dlaf walk of one particle. Far from the cluster it jumps by its distance to the cluster, then it steps until it is near enough to join. It nearly always joins a tip.

Which dlaf hook makes which coral

StaghornshouldJoin refuses a join on a busy particle or from below, so tips grow up. Low AttractionDistance, high Stickiness.
Sea fanmotionVector and randomStartingPosition keep the walk in a vertical plane across the current.
TableshouldJoin lets a short stalk grow up, then only horizontal joins stick; placeParticle flattens the plate.
BrainHigh Stubbornness; placeParticle lays each polyp on a compact dome (accretive growth).
FingerLow Stickiness and a slow fall: walkers creep in, so the lobes get thick.
AllrandomStartingPosition puts the snow on a disc above the colony, upstream; motionVector adds the fall and the current; shouldReset starts again at the seabed or out of the reef box.

From particles to a living surface

Each joined particle is a round cone from its parent, as thick as the branch it feeds: the radius grows with the number of particles above it (the pipe rule), so trunks are thick and tips are thin and round. A smooth minimum blends the cones into one signed distance field. The field lives in small bricks, and only the bricks near a new particle are recomputed and meshed again (surface nets, in a worker), so the coral grows live. Each species adds its own shape term: a web in the plane of a sea fan, a flat plate for a table coral, meandering valleys on a brain coral. Polyps sit on the surface, more at the tips, and open and close in the current.

The rock

The reef rock is a seeded signed distance field: winding canyons with undercut walls, bridges and free arches, pinnacles, caves, ledges and scree, with strata and crag detail. It is meshed with surface nets and textured with scanned CC0 stone. The colonies seed on exposed rock and grow on it; a walker that touches the rock near a colony base can start a new root.

The dive

The plot is a circle in deep water. Past its edge the rock and the sand go into the black over 30% of the radius. The light comes from the ROV lamps: each lamp has a cone in the turbid water, the lead lamp casts shadows, and the marine snow shows in the beams. A faint blue light comes down from the surface, with caustics: the sun refracted by a random sea of 28 waves (swell, wind sea and chop), so the net of light is irregular, and it breaks into patches that come and go.

The camera card shows the lead ROV camera sent over a slow-scan TV link with the encoder and decoder of the SSTV page of this site: Robot 36 or Martin M1, with noise, fades and a little slant.

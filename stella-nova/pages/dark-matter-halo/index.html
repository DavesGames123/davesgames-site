Dark matter in the Milky Way: where the missing mass is

The Sun goes around the centre of the Galaxy at about 230 km/s. Add up every star and gas cloud that we can see, work out how hard they pull, and they can hold the Sun on its orbit at only about 177 km/s. Farther out the gap gets wider: at 20 kpc from the centre the stars still move at about 200 km/s, where the visible matter alone would allow about 125. The extra pull comes from mass that gives no light. Its estimated shape is a round, diffuse halo that reaches far beyond the disc of stars. How much of it there is, and how it is spread out near the centre, is still argued over.

The curves come from the Milky Way mass model of McMillan (2017). The dots are measured speeds of real stars. The halo controls at the top let you change the halo; every plot follows. Items tagged illustrative show a shape or an idea, not data.

01

Too fast: the rotation curve

A rotation curve is the speed of a circular orbit at each distance from the centre. Each part of the Galaxy adds to the pull, and the speeds of the parts add as squares. Switch off the dark halo and see what the visible matter alone can do.

Past the edge of the mass, the speed would fall as r−1/2, the Keplerian law of the planets. The visible matter of the Galaxy is mostly inside 15 kpc, so without a halo the curve would follow that fall.

02

Where the dark matter is

The visible matter is a thin disc and a small bulge. The dark matter is a round cloud around them. Near the Sun it is thin: about 0.01 solar masses per cubic parsec, less than one proton mass per two cubic centimetres. But it fills a volume thousands of times larger than the disc.

03

The estimates disagree, mostly at the centre and the edge

A rotation curve fixes the mass inside each radius well. It does not fix the shape of the halo. Four common halo families are drawn here, all set to the same density at the Sun. They are families of curves, not fits to the Milky Way: each one could be tuned to match the data near the Sun, and they still disagree by factors of ten or more at the centre.

Density against distance

profile families, same local density

The gold bar at the Sun spans the measured local density, 0.30 to 0.38 GeV/cm³. Inside 1 kpc the cusp of NFW climbs while the cores of Burkert and the isothermal sphere stay flat.

Rotation curve with each halo family

families, not fits

Same visible matter (dashed gold), four halos. The grey band is the spread between them. At the Sun they agree by construction. Where the measured points stop, near 25 kpc, they already differ, and past that nothing ties them down. That is one reason why the published total masses span a factor of about seven.

Published estimates

from the papers

The masses are not all the same quantity. "Total" includes the visible matter. "Virial" is the mass inside the radius where the mean density is 200 times the critical density of the universe. Jiao et al. find a curve that falls almost as the Keplerian law beyond about 19 kpc, which leaves room for much less dark matter far out.

The four families

NFW. The shape found in cold dark matter simulations. A cusp: the density keeps rising as 1/r toward the centre.

Einasto. Fits newer simulations a little better. The slope gets shallower toward the centre; here α = 0.17.

Burkert. A flat core. Fits the rotation curves of many dwarf galaxies.

Cored isothermal. The oldest model. Gives a flat rotation curve far out, so its mass grows without limit.

04

How much of the mass is dark

Draw a sphere around the centre and ask what share of the mass inside it is dark. Near the centre the stars of the bulge and disc win. At the Sun's radius it is about half and half. Far out the halo dominates, and the share climbs toward the cosmic average that the Planck satellite measured from the early universe.

The numbers

Why the evidence is strongest far out, not at the Sun: at the Sun the visible matter already gives about three quarters of the speed, so the halo needed there depends on how well we know the mass of the disc. At 20 kpc and beyond the visible matter cannot come close.

05

How it is measured

Nobody sees the halo. Its mass is read from how things move in it, and each method works over its own range of distance.

Neuroscience · reinforcement learning · a model, not a fly

Two fruit fly brains learn a card duel

A fruit fly learns which smells mean sugar and which mean a shock with a small structure called the mushroom body. Here two copies of that circuit, wired from the published connectome, see a card game as if it were a smell, and learn to play it against each other by the same rule a fly uses: dopamine tells a synapse whether the last choice turned out better or worse than expected.

    01

    The game: a small card duel of our own

    The flies play a cut-down duel in the style of the trading card games: two players, 20 life each, a 40-card deck, lands that make mana, creatures that attack and block, and spells. Every card, name, picture and mana glyph here is our own. The rules are short on purpose, so that each decision is a choice among a few options.

    One turn

    rules engine: game/rules.js
    1. Untap and draw. Your lands and creatures stand up and you draw one card. The first player skips the draw on turn one.
    2. Main. Play at most one land, then cast any cards you can pay for. Lands tap for mana by themselves.
    3. Attack. Pick which ready creatures attack, as one choice.
    4. Block. The other player picks, as one choice, which creature blocks which attacker. Unblocked attackers hit the player.
    5. End. Damage wears off. Above seven cards in hand, discard the cheapest.

    A player at 0 life, or who must draw from an empty deck, loses. After turn 60 the game is a draw. To keep each choice short, the engine lists at most 16 attack plans and 16 block plans, and always includes "do nothing".

      02

      The fly's learning centre

      The mushroom body is a paired structure in the insect brain where smells become memories. Odour information arrives on projection neurons (PNs). Each Kenyon cell (KC) samples a few PNs at random-looking claws. KCs talk to a small set of output neurons (MBONs) in separate compartments, and dopamine neurons (DANs) that carry reward or punishment signals reach exactly those compartments. That layout is the whole learning machine. In numbers: each mushroom body has about two thousand KCs, read out by a few dozen MBONs of about twenty types in about fifteen compartments, each fed by its own DAN types.

        The smell of a board

        A fly cannot read cards. So each board becomes an odour: 48 numbers that describe it (life totals, lands, creatures, power on each side and so on) each drive a fixed set of glomeruli, the input channels of the fly's antennal lobe, through tuning curves. Similar boards give similar smells. Pick a feature and move it to see the pattern change.

          A sparse code: few Kenyon cells speak at once

          Each Kenyon cell sums a handful of PN inputs, and one large inhibitory neuron, APL, feeds back onto all of them, so only the most strongly driven few fire: in the fly, about a small share of KCs respond to a given odour. The model keeps the same share active. The result is a sparse code: two smells that overlap a lot on the PNs share far fewer active KCs. That keeps memories of similar boards apart.           05

          Dopamine teaches

          Fly learning is a three-factor rule. A KC-to-MBON synapse changes only when the KC was active (factor one and two: pre and post) and a dopamine neuron in that compartment fires (factor three). The change is a depression. Reward-signalling PAM neurons depress synapses onto output neurons that drive avoidance; punishment-signalling PPL1 neurons depress synapses onto output neurons that drive approach. Our model sends each DAN the temporal-difference error δ, the surprise of the last move, the same signal that dopamine neurons carry in monkeys.

          06

          Train them yourself

          Start from naive flies, with all KC-to-MBON weights at their start value, and train them in a background thread on your device. Each training game is drawn from a mix: half fly against fly, some against a simple greedy player and some against a random player. The curves are live numbers from your browser: the share of recent training games each fly won, with exploration on. "Evaluate" measures them properly, with exploration and learning off.

          What happens per game

          • Each fly scores every legal move by the value of the board it would lead to, and picks by a softmax at temperature a small value while training (the best move when watching).
          • For an attack, the board after the attack still waits for the opponent's block. The fly scores it by the worst block the opponent could make. This look-ahead is a model step, not fly biology.
          • After each of its moves, the fly computes δ and its DANs update the KC-to-MBON weights of the KCs that were recently active.
          • At the end, the win (+1), loss (−1) or draw (0) is the only reward. Nothing tells the fly which cards are good.
          • The wiring (PN to KC claws, which DAN reaches which compartment) never changes. Only the KC-to-MBON weights learn.

            09

            What this is not

            It is easy to oversell a project like this. Here is the plain account.

            Not a real fly

            No animal was trained. A real fly would never see a card game. We took the wiring diagram of one circuit and ran a rate model of it on a computer. Real neurons spike, adapt, release several transmitters and change in ways our model leaves out.

            Not the whole brain

            We model one mushroom body. A whole-brain model of the fly built from the full connectome exists and predicts real feeding and grooming circuits, but it is not trained to play anything. The mushroom body is the part of the fly that is known to learn from dopamine, which is why we chose it.

            Not how a fly sees a game

            The 48 board features, the way they become glomerulus rates, the TD error sent to the DANs, the one-step look-ahead with the imagined block and the game itself are our design choices. They are labelled "model" wherever they appear. The connectome fixes who talks to whom, not what the signals mean here.

            Why it still says something

            The learning rule here is not ours: dopamine-gated depression of KC-to-MBON synapses has been measured in the fly, and the sparse KC code and its compartments are in the wiring data. Showing that such a circuit, with its real fan-out and sparsity, can learn a value for boards of a game it has never seen is a small demonstration of how general that design is. Self-play with TD learning is an old idea in games; the new part is the wiring.

            Limits we know about

            • The flies look one move ahead, plus the opponent's block after an attack. They do not plan further.
            • Both flies share the same PN-to-KC claws; they differ in the feature-to-glomerulus map and in what they learned.
            • The duel is far simpler than any commercial card game: no stack, no instants, one block per attacker.
            • Win rates are against weak fixed bots and each other. We make no claim of skill against people.

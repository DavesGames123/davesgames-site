    Two fruit fly brains learn a card duel

    A fruit fly learns which smells mean sugar and which mean a shock with a small structure called the mushroom body. Here two copies of that circuit, each wired from the published connectome of a different fly, see a card game as if it were a smell, and learn to play it against each other with a rule modelled on the fly's: dopamine changes the synapses of the cells that were just active. In our model, the dopamine signal tells those synapses whether the last choice turned out better or worse than expected.

    projection neurons (the board's smell) active Kenyon cells approach output / PAM avoid output / PPL1

    We do not train a real fly. We train two computer models. Each model is one mushroom body: projection neurons, Kenyon cells, output neurons and dopamine neurons, taken from a published fruit fly connectome. Fly A and Fly B are wired from two different animals: Fly A from the hemibrain connectome, Fly B from the FlyWire connectome. Fly A wears the cyan collar, Fly B the magenta collar; the flies are drawn by us, larger than life, and only the brain maps come from data. On the table both flies play with their trained weights and learning is switched off. The neural panel in each scorecard shows the Kenyon cells of the move the fly chose, and the dopamine signal the model computes after it. Parts marked model are our simplifications; parts marked illustrative are teaching toys. The card game is our own, inspired by Magic: The Gathering.     01

    The game: a small card duel of our own

    The flies play a cut-down duel in the style of the trading card games: two players, 20 life each, a 40-card deck, lands that make mana of five colours (Amber, Moss, Dew, Rot and Pollen), creatures that attack and block, and spells. Each colour has its own deck, and the mixed decks join two or three colours with dual lands. Every card, name, card text and mana glyph here is our own. The pictures are crops of public-domain natural-history plates and paintings (credits under Sources). The rules are short on purpose, so that each decision is a choice among a few options.

    One turn

    rules engine: game/rules.js
    1. Untap and draw. Your lands and creatures stand up and you draw one card. The first player skips the draw on turn one.
    2. Main. Play at most one land, then cast any cards you can pay for. Lands tap for mana by themselves.
    3. Attack. Pick which ready creatures attack, as one choice.
    4. Block. The other player picks, as one choice, which creature blocks which attacker. Unblocked attackers hit the player.
    5. End. Damage wears off. Above seven cards in hand, discard the cheapest.

    A player at 0 life, or who must draw from an empty deck, loses. After turn 60 the game is a draw. To keep each choice short, the engine lists at most 16 attack plans and 16 block plans, and always includes "do nothing".

    Mana on the table. Each land makes one mana of its colour; a dual land makes one of its two. Lands of one kind stand in one stack. A bright land is untapped; a dim, turned land is tapped. To cast a card, the fly touches each land it pays with and a token goes from the land to the card. A token that pays a generic cost turns into a grey square on the way.

      02

      The fly's learning centre

      The mushroom body is a paired structure in the insect brain where smells become memories. Odour information arrives on projection neurons (PNs). Each Kenyon cell (KC) samples a few PNs at random-looking claws. KCs talk to a small set of output neurons (MBONs) in separate compartments, and dopamine neurons (DANs) that carry reward or punishment signals reach exactly those compartments. That layout is the whole learning machine. In numbers: each mushroom body has about two thousand KCs, read out by a few dozen MBONs of about twenty types in about fifteen compartments, each fed by its own DAN types.

        03

        The smell of a board

        A fly cannot read cards. So each board becomes an odour: 48 numbers that describe it (life totals, lands, creatures, power on each side and so on) act as receptor types. In the fly, each receptor type sends its signal to one glomerulus, an input channel of the antennal lobe. So in the model each number drives its own glomerulus through a dose-response curve, and the spare glomeruli get a second receptor type. All projection neurons of one glomerulus carry the same rate. Similar boards give similar smells. Pick a feature and move it to see the pattern change.

          04

          A sparse code: few Kenyon cells speak at once

          Each Kenyon cell sums a handful of PN inputs, and one large inhibitory neuron, APL, feeds back onto all of them, so only the most strongly driven few fire: in the fly, about 5–10% of KCs respond to a given odour, and a KC that responds fires only a few spikes. KC responses are this selective because few PNs converge on each KC and its firing threshold is high. The model lets the top 10% of KCs fire, the top of that band (the same share in each fly). Each winner gets a graded rate: how far its drive is above the best KC that did not win, which stands for the APL level. The result is a sparse code: two smells that overlap a lot on the PNs share far fewer active KCs. That keeps memories of similar boards apart.           05

          Dopamine teaches

          In the fly, a KC-to-MBON synapse changes when the KC was active and a dopamine neuron in that compartment fires, and the order of the two signals matters. The change is a depression. The model uses the same two signals: a decaying trace of the activity of each KC, and the dopamine signal of the compartment. It uses no MBON activity. Reward-signalling PAM neurons depress synapses onto output neurons that drive avoidance; punishment-signalling PPL1 neurons depress synapses onto output neurons that drive approach. Our model sends each DAN the temporal-difference error δ, the surprise of the last move, the same signal that dopamine neurons carry in monkeys.

          06

          Train them yourself

          Start from naive flies, with all KC-to-MBON weights at their start value, and train them in a background thread on your device. Each training game is drawn from a mix: half fly against fly, some against a simple greedy player and some against a random player. The curves are live numbers from your browser: the share of recent training games each fly won, with exploration on. "Evaluate" measures them properly, with exploration and learning off.

          What happens per game

          • Each fly scores every legal move by the value of the board it would lead to, and picks by a softmax at temperature a small value while training (the best move when watching).
          • For an attack, the board after the attack still waits for the opponent's block. The fly scores it by the worst block the opponent could make. This look-ahead is a model step, not fly biology.
          • After each of its moves, the fly computes δ and its DANs update the KC-to-MBON weights of the KCs that were recently active.
          • At the end, the win (+1), loss (−1) or draw (0) is the only reward. Nothing tells the fly which cards are good.
          • The wiring (PN to KC claws, which DAN reaches which compartment) never changes. Only the KC-to-MBON weights learn.

          The trained weights from this panel are used by section 07 when you press "New game" there. The table and play mode keep the shipped weights.

            How good are they?

            The numbers here were measured by our own training code, offline, with a fixed seed, and stored with the command that made them. Nothing in the curve or the table is typed in by hand. A random player is a low bar; the greedy player is a short hand-written heuristic, not a strong opponent.

            • Win rates near 50% against each other are expected: the two flies trained together. A draw (turn cap) is not a win.
            • The shipped flies are the checkpoint with the best result against the greedy bot. Choosing on those games makes their checkpoint numbers a little too good, so the table uses new games with a different seed.

            • Why the graded KC code at 10%: in a development run with tools/mb-lab.mjs (3 seeds, 3000 held-out games against the greedy bot), flies with the graded code at 10% won 30.0% of the games (899 of 3000), and flies with the binary code at 5% won 21.7% (652 of 3000). That run is not the shipped training run.
            09

            What this is not

            It is easy to oversell a project like this. Here is the plain account.

            Not a real fly

            No animal was trained. A real fly would never see a card game. We took the wiring diagram of one circuit from two flies and ran a rate model of it on a computer. Real neurons spike, adapt, release several transmitters and change in ways our model leaves out.

            Not the whole brain

            We model one mushroom body. A whole-brain model of the fly built from the full connectome exists and predicts real feeding and grooming circuits, but it is not trained to play anything. The mushroom body is the part of the fly that is known to learn from dopamine, which is why we chose it.

            Not how a fly sees a game

            The 48 board features, the way they become glomerulus rates, the TD error sent to the DANs, the one-step look-ahead with the imagined block and the game itself are our design choices. They are labelled "model" wherever they appear. The connectome fixes who talks to whom, not what the signals mean here.

            Why it still says something

            The learning rule here is not ours: dopamine-gated depression of KC-to-MBON synapses has been measured in the fly, and the KC fan-out and the compartments are in the wiring data. Showing that such a circuit, with its real fan-out and a fly-like share of active KCs, can learn a value for boards of a game it has never seen is a small demonstration of how general that design is. Self-play with TD learning is an old idea in games; the new part is the wiring.

            Limits we know about

            • The flies look one move ahead, plus the opponent's block after an attack. They do not plan further.
            • Fly A and Fly B are wired from two different animals, so their PN-to-KC claws and their cell counts differ. Each fly also has its own feature-to-glomerulus map and its own learned weights. The two data sets detect synapses by different methods, so we do not read a difference between the flies as biology only.
            • The duel is far simpler than any commercial card game: no stack, no instants, one block per attacker.
            • Win rates are against weak fixed bots and each other. We make no claim of skill against people.

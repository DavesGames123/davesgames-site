Conway's Game of Life

A grid of square cells, each one alive or dead. At each tick of the clock, every cell counts its live neighbours and follows four rules. Nobody plays it: you set the first pattern, and the rules do the rest.

RulesOne stepPatternsHistoryWhy it matters

The four rules

Each cell has eight neighbours: the cells that touch it on a side or a corner. This is the Moore neighbourhood. All cells change at the same time, from the counts of the generation before.

Short form: B3/S23. A dead cell is Born with exactly 3 live neighbours. A live cell Survives with 2 or 3. Every other cell is dead in the next generation.

Kinds of pattern

Most random starts end as a scatter of small stable shapes. People sort the shapes by what they do over time.

Garden of Eden

A pattern that no pattern can make: it has no parent, so it can only appear as a start. The Garden of Eden theorem of Edward Moore and John Myhill (1962–63) showed that such patterns exist. In 1971 Roger Banks and others at MIT found the first explicit one for Life.

History

  1. 1970 John Conway, at the University of Cambridge, tries many rules on a Go board and chooses B3/S23. It is hard to predict: some patterns die, some settle, and some seem to grow.
  2. 1970 Martin Gardner describes the game in his Mathematical Games column in Scientific American (October 1970). Readers everywhere start to run it by hand and on early computers.
  3. 1970 Richard Guy finds the glider. Conway offers a $50 prize for a pattern whose population grows without limit.
  4. 1970 Bill Gosper and his group at MIT win the prize with the glider gun. It fires a glider every 30 generations, forever.
  5. 1982 Elwyn Berlekamp, Conway and Guy show in Winning Ways how streams of gliders can build a computer.
  6. 2000 Paul Rendell builds a working Turing machine in Life.
  7. 2010 Andrew Wade's Gemini, the first spaceship that copies itself.
  8. 2016 The copperhead, the first c/10 spaceship, found by a searcher called zdr.

Why it matters

Emergence

No rule mentions gliders, guns or oscillators. Each cell only counts its neighbours. Still, structures appear that move, interact and last, and we describe them with words the rules never use. Life is a small, exact example of complex behaviour that comes from simple local rules.

Life can compute

A stream of gliders can carry bits: a glider is 1, a gap is 0. Aim a gun across an input stream so that gliders that meet destroy each other. The gun gliders that get through are then the NOT of the input. From there, AND and OR gates, memory and a clock (a gun) follow. So Life is Turing complete: it can run any program, given enough space. One result follows from this: no algorithm can decide, for every start, whether the pattern will die out.

A family of automata

A cellular automaton is a grid of cells, a set of states and a local rule. John von Neumann designed one in the 1940s with 29 states that can copy itself. Stephen Wolfram studied the 256 elementary automata: one row of cells, two states, three neighbours. One of them, Rule 110, is also Turing complete (Matthew Cook, published 2004).

Life is one of the 218 = 262,144 Life-like rules: two states, the Moore neighbourhood, and any set of birth counts and survival counts. Try HighLife, Seeds, Day & Night or Maze in the rule picker. They behave very differently.

From Life to Lenia

Let each cell hold a number from 0 to 1 instead of on or off, let it look at a smooth ring of neighbours, and let time move in small steps. The result is Lenia, a continuous cousin of Life. Its creatures glide and turn like small animals.

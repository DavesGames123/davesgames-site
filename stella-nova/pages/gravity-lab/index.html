What am I looking at?

Every body pulls on every other with Newton’s law of gravity, . Nothing is scripted: the computer adds up the pulls and takes small steps in time.

The bodies (stars, planets, moons, black holes, galaxy cores) are computed in double precision with a 6th-order integrator that takes smaller steps in close passes. The particles (dust, debris, the stars of a galaxy disk) feel the bodies but are too light to pull back; thousands of them move on the GPU. In the disk experiment every particle pulls on every other.

Pick something from the tray and throw it in: a planet, a star, a black hole, a swarm of rocks. A tap gives a circular orbit; a drag throws it. The dashed line shows its path.

Randomize mixes the 22 experiments, adds intruders on computed paths, and generates new scenes. Each roll has a seed in the link, so you can share it.

Simplifications
    Method

    Bodies: f64 on the CPU, Yoshida's 6th-order composition of the leapfrog, adaptive substeps (a fraction of the shortest pair time), perfect mergers on contact, breakup inside the fluid Roche limit. Particles: kick-drift-kick leapfrog against the body positions of each step, one GPU thread each; the self-gravity disk uses a softened direct sum over all pairs. node tests.mjs checks the integrators against Kepler, the figure-eight, Burrau's problem, the Lagrange points, and the GPU kernels against the CPU.

    Particle counts here (2 048 to about 36 000) are far below research codes, which run millions to billions of particles. Particles that feel the bodies are massless tracers; galaxy cores are softened point masses with no dark-matter halo; bodies merge perfectly on contact.

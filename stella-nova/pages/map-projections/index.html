Why every flat map lies

Peel an orange and press the peel flat: it tears or stretches. A map has the same problem, and in 1827 Carl Friedrich Gauss proved that no cleverness can fix it. His Theorema Egregium ("remarkable theorem") says that the curvature of a surface can be measured from inside the surface, by distances alone. A map that kept every distance would also keep the curvature. A sphere of radius has curvature

so no flat map can keep all distances. You can see the curvature in a triangle: on a globe the angles of a triangle of area add up to more than 180°,

A triangle from the North Pole down two meridians 90° apart to the equator has three right angles. On paper that is impossible.

So every map chooses what to keep. In 1859 Nicolas Auguste Tissot found a way to see the choice: draw small circles of equal size on the globe and look at them on the map. A conformal map has everywhere (circles stay circles). An equal-area map has everywhere (circles keep their area). Gauss's theorem says no map can have everywhere.

Mercator and the Greenland problem

In 1569 Gerardus Mercator drew a world map for sailors. He spaced the parallels so that the map is conformal: at every point the north-south scale equals the east-west scale, which is stretched by . The result is

The area scale is 1 at the equator, 2 at 45°, 4 at 60° and about 10.5 at 72°, the middle of Greenland. Greenland covers about 2.2 million km²; Africa covers about 30.4 million km², fourteen times more. On Mercator they look about the same size. Russia looks bigger than all of Africa (17.1 against 30.4 million km²), and Antarctica becomes an endless white band, because the pole is infinitely far away. Web maps stop at 85.05°.

None of this was a mistake. Mercator's map was made to keep angles, and it does that perfectly. The problem starts when a navigation chart is used as a picture of the world.

Equal-area vs conformal

Because no map can keep both angles and areas, the two big families of maps choose one. A conformal map keeps angles, so small shapes look right: a city block is square, a coastline has its true wiggles. An equal-area map keeps areas, so every square kilometre gets the same space on the paper:

Use a conformal map when direction and local shape matter: navigation, weather fronts, street maps, surveying grids (UTM, State Plane). Use an equal-area map when the map shows how much of something there is: population density, forests, crops, election results. A density map on Mercator makes northern countries look far more important than they are.

Turn on the angle heatmap to see the price each family pays. A conformal map is dark everywhere (no angle error) but its area heatmap changes colour. An equal-area map is the reverse.

The compromise maps

For a general world map, most cartographers now prefer to keep nothing exactly and spread the error out instead. In 1963 Arthur Robinson drew a map by eye: he adjusted the length and spacing of the parallels until the continents "looked right", then published the result as a table every 5°. Studies that score world maps on area, angle and distance errors together put the Winkel tripel near the top. Tom Patterson's Natural Earth map (2007, polynomials 2011) aims for the same balance with flat poles. Equal Earth (2018) takes the Robinson look and makes it exactly equal-area, so a thematic map can look familiar and be fair.

Maps for navigation and for airlines

Two lines matter to a navigator. A rhumb line crosses every meridian at the same angle, so you can follow it with one compass setting. On Mercator it is straight, which is why Mercator made his map. A great circle is the shortest path on the sphere; its bearing changes all the way. On the gnomonic map, a plane touching the globe and lit from its centre, every great circle is straight.

The classic method combines them: draw the straight route on a gnomonic chart, copy points along it onto a Mercator chart, and steer the short rhumb lines between them. From New York to Tokyo the great circle is 10,849 km on a sphere (10,873 km on the WGS84 ellipsoid) and passes near Alaska; the rhumb line is 12,787 km. Airlines fly close to great circles, adjusted for winds; that is why a flight path looks curved on a flat map.

The azimuthal equidistant map centred on a city gives true distance and direction from that city to everywhere on Earth: the reason it is used for radio, range rings and the United Nations emblem.

Web Mercator and why your phone uses it

When Google Maps launched in 2005 it chose a Mercator map of a sphere, and nearly every web map followed. The reasons are practical. It is conformal, so streets meet at their true angles and a roundabout is round at every zoom. North is always up.

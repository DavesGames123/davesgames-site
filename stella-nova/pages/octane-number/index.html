Octane number: why some fuels knock

The number on a petrol pump is not a measure of energy. Every gasoline carries about the same energy per litre. The octane number says how well the fuel waits: how long the unburned mixture in the cylinder can be squeezed and heated before it ignites by itself, ahead of the flame from the spark plug. When it does not wait, the engine knocks. The scale runs between two molecules: n-heptane, a straight chain of seven carbons that ignites easily, and iso-octane, a compact, branched chain of eight that does not.

The 3D molecules are the views of the Molecule Explorer: drag to turn them. The engine on this page is a model with one zone of gas; it shows the mechanism and the trends, not a particular engine. Values tagged measured come from the sources at the end.

01

The scale is a mixture

An octane number is the percentage of iso-octane in a blend with n-heptane. To rate a fuel, a test engine runs on it and its compression is raised until it knocks at a standard strength. Then blends of the two reference fuels are tried until one knocks the same way. A gasoline that matches 91 % iso-octane and 9 % heptane is 91 octane, whatever it is made of.

Two tests, two numbers

ASTM D2699, D2700

Both tests use the same CFR engine: one cylinder, 3.25 in bore and 4.5 in stroke, with a head that moves to change the compression ratio while it runs. The Motor test is the harder one: a hotter mixture and a faster engine. Most fuels rate lower on it. The working range of the tests is about 40 to 120; numbers outside 0-100 are found with blends of the fuel in a reference fuel, or with lead added to iso-octane.

02

Knock: the end gas ignites by itself

The spark lights a flame that takes a few milliseconds to cross the chamber. As it burns, the hot gas behind it expands and squeezes the unburned mixture ahead of it, the end gas, to higher pressure and temperature. Hot, compressed fuel and air react on their own after a delay. If the delay runs out before the flame arrives, the end gas burns all at once. The pressure jumps and rings at the acoustic frequency of the cylinder: the metallic ping of knock. Heavy knock erodes pistons.

An earlier spark puts the peak pressure closer to top centre and gives more work, up to a point. It also heats the end gas sooner. The red band is where the chosen fuel knocks. A knock sensor in a modern engine hears the ring and moves the spark later, so a low-octane fuel costs power instead of pistons.

03

Why the shape of the molecule matters

Knock starts with chemistry at 700-900 K, cooler than a flame. A radical pulls a hydrogen atom off a fuel molecule; oxygen adds to the carbon that is left; the peroxy group then bends back and takes another hydrogen from further along the same molecule. That internal step, through a six- or seven-membered ring, leads to a branch in the chain: one radical becomes three. Long chains of CH2 groups have many weak hydrogens in the right places. Branches, rings and alcohols do not.

    04

    RON, MON and the number on the pump

    A fuel gets two numbers because the two tests stress it differently. The difference, RON − MON, is the sensitivity. Paraffins have almost none. Alcohols and aromatics have a lot: they resist knock very well in a cool, slow engine and less well in a hot, fast one. Modern turbocharged engines with direct injection run cooler end gas than the Motor test, so for them RON tells more than MON.

    A short history

    Knock limited the compression of engines from the start, and with it their efficiency. The answers came in two kinds: add something that stops the chain, or make molecules that do not start it.

Ramanujan–Sato series

In 1914 Ramanujan wrote down series for 1/π that gain about eight digits with each term. They all have one shape: an integer sequence s(k), a linear part and a power.

Series digits per term

Race

Correct digits are −log₁₀ |πn − π|, rounded down, for the value πn after n terms. The chart stops a series at 3000 digits (the dot).

A decimal is fixed by term t when the value after t terms, and after every later term, agrees with π up to that decimal. Blue and gold change at each term; the orange mark is the first decimal not fixed yet. The faint digits are the current value, still wrong.

Each bar multiplies or divides by a power of ten. s(k) grows by a fixed factor per term, Ck grows faster, and the term is what is left. Hollow dots are negative terms.

Binary splitting: the terms are multiplied in a balanced tree of exact integers, then one division and one square root give all the digits.

Why so few terms

Each term is smaller than the one before by an almost fixed factor. The ratio tends to a constant g, so each term adds about correct digits. For the Chudnovsky series C = 640320³ and g = 12³ = 1728, which gives 14.18 digits per term. Ramanujan’s 1914 series has C = 396⁴ and g = 256: 7.98 digits per term.

The constants are not chosen by search. C is the value of a modular function at a special point τ. At level 1 the function is Klein’s j-invariant,

and at τ = (1 + √−163)/2 it is exactly −640320³. It is an integer because 163 is a Heegner number: the field ℚ(√−163) has class number one. There q = −e−π√163 is tiny, so the series for j almost stops after 744:

The higher levels use other modular functions (eta quotients for levels 2 to 11) and other integer sequences: binomial products, the Apéry, Franel and Domb numbers, and sums of fourth powers. A larger C gives a faster series.

Ramanujan gave levels 1 to 4A in 1914 without proofs and spoke of “corresponding theories”. The Borweins and Bailey proved his family in 1989; the Chudnovsky brothers proved theirs in 1989, and it later gave 10 trillion digits (2011). H. H. Chan and S. Cooper found a general method in 2012.

How this page computes

Every number is an exact integer (JavaScript BigInt). A value with W decimals is kept as the integer value × 10W. Square roots come from an integer square root with Newton steps. Every series is summed term by term with twelve guard digits, and the value of π after each term is compared with a stored copy of the first 10000 decimals.

That copy is checked in the page tests against two computations that share no code: the Chudnovsky series by binary splitting and Machin’s arctangent formula. Compute runs in a worker, so the page stays live.

About

The satellite is a pile of grains that touch. Each grain feels the gravity of every other grain, the planet, and the push, friction and cement of the grains it touches. Nothing else holds the pile together, so the run, not a formula, decides if it survives.

Roche's limit compares two pulls at the satellite: the tide, the difference of the planet's gravity across the body, and the body's own gravity. A rigid sphere comes apart where the tide at its surface equals its surface gravity, at 1.26 Rp(ρp/ρs)1/3. A fluid body stretches first, which makes the tide stronger, so it fails farther out, at 2.44. A real rubble pile has friction and some cement, so its limit falls between the two.

Inside the limit the satellite stretches along the line to the planet and sheds grains from its two tips (the Lagrange points L1 and L2). The grains go onto orbits a little faster or slower than the satellite, and the difference winds the stream into a ring. Outside the limit, shed grains fall back together: this is how the comet Shoemaker-Levy 9 became a string of fragments after Jupiter pulled it apart in 1992.

Method. N up to 32k grains in WebGPU compute. Contacts every step (spring-dashpot, a friction spring with a Coulomb cap, rolling resistance, cohesion), self-gravity by a direct sum every 32 steps with a linear extrapolation between sums, the planet as a point mass with J2, kick-drift-kick steps. tests.mjs checks the GPU forces against a CPU reference and checks the formulas against published values. The satellite is drawn larger than real moons (Rs/Rp = 0.12); the Roche limit does not depend on its size. The ring glow multiplies the optical depth of the few thousand shed grains by the gain above.

Sphere tracing

A signed distance field d(p) says how far p is from the nearest surface. A ray can step that far and never pass through anything.

How it works

The step. At the ray point, d is the radius of a sphere that holds no surface. So the ray can move by d and stay safe (Hart 1996).

On the slice, each circle is that sphere cut by the plane. A circle touches the contour where the nearest surface point lies in the plane. Move the plane off the ray, and the touch points leave the slice.

Bounds. Booleans, smooth unions and folds of space often give a field that is only a lower bound of the distance. That is still safe, but the steps get short. A warp such as a twist can make |∇d| larger than 1. Then a full step can pass through the surface. Divide the step by the Lipschitz constant L to fix it.

Over-relaxation. Keinert et al. 2014 step by ω d with ω > 1. If the new sphere does not overlap the old one, the big step may have skipped a surface, so the tracer goes back and uses ω = 1. Open the compare view to see the step counts side by side. Relaxation pays off when d is less than the true distance: after a bound operator, or with a step scale below 1. On an exact field the plain step is already the best step, so ω > 1 only adds failed steps.

Smooth union. A polynomial blend of two fields adds a fillet of width k (Quilez).

Soft shadows. March from the surface toward the light. The smallest ratio of d to the distance so far measures how close the ray came to a blocker, which gives the penumbra (Quilez).

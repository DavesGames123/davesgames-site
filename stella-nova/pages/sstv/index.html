Slow-scan television: a picture sent as sound

A slow-scan picture travels as a whistle. Each pixel is a tone between 1500 Hz for black and 2300 Hz for white, and a short 1200 Hz pulse starts every line. Any voice radio can carry it, from a handheld to the International Space Station. The monitor below decodes real audio as it arrives and paints the lines one by one.

Every figure here runs our own JavaScript encoder and decoder in your browser. The encoder follows the published mode timings and pysstv by Andras Veres-Szentkiralyi, the Python SSTV generator this page was built around. The decoder is ours. Jump to the receiver to send your own picture.

01

Pictures down a voice channel

Broadcast television needs a channel several megahertz wide. A voice channel is about 3 kHz. Slow-scan television fits a picture into that 3 kHz by taking its time: seconds or minutes for one frame instead of a thirtieth of a second.

  1. 1957Copthorne Macdonald, a student at the University of Kentucky, builds a system that sends a still picture in about 8 seconds over an amateur voice channel. A long-persistence P7 tube holds the picture on screen. He describes it in QST in 1958.
  2. 1960sRadio amateurs build 8-second, 120-line black-and-white systems. Line rate and frame time are chosen from the 60 Hz mains, so a picture is 120 lines at 15 lines per second. That format lives on as Robot 8 BW.
  3. 1969The Apollo 11 camera on the Moon uses its own slow-scan format, 320 lines at 10 frames per second. That is much faster than amateur SSTV but still too slow for broadcast, so NASA converts it on the ground for television.
  4. 1970sRobot Research sells slow-scan monitors and cameras. Scan converters with digital memory replace the fading P7 tube, so longer frames and colour become practical.
  5. 1980s to 1990sColour modes arrive: Robot 36 and 72 (luminance and colour differences), Martin (Martin Emmerson, G3OQD) and Scottie (Eddie Murphy, GM3SBC) in Britain, Wraase in Germany, and later the PD modes of Paul Turner, G4IJE, and Don Rotier, K0HEO.
  6. 2000JL Barber, N7CXI, collects the timings in "Proposal for SSTV mode specifications" for the Dayton SSTV forum. PC sound cards now do the whole job in software.
  7. Since about 2008Cosmonauts and ARISS volunteers send series of SSTV pictures from the International Space Station on 145.800 MHz FM, mostly in PD120 and PD180. A handheld radio and a phone app are enough to receive them.
02

Sound as frequency

SSTV is frequency modulation of an audio tone. The receiver does not care how loud the tone is, only its pitch. The pitch maps linearly to brightness, so a radio fade does not darken the picture.

03

The VIS header: which mode is coming

Before the picture, the sender plays a vertical interval signalling (VIS) code: a 1900 Hz leader, a 10 ms break, the leader again, then 10 bits of 30 ms. The seven data bits name the mode. A parity bit catches a bit flipped by noise.

Decoded bit by bit

The trace is the demodulated frequency of the synthesized header, slowed down about 5 times. The decoder takes the mean frequency over the middle of each 30 ms bit: below 1200 Hz is a 1, above is a 0.

Seven bits and a parity bit

Bits go least significant first. Parity is even: the eight bits always hold an even number of ones. Martin M1 is 44, Scottie S1 is 60, Robot 36 is 8 and PD-120 is 95.

The leader also tunes the receiver. If the radio is off frequency, the 1900 Hz leader shows the error, and the decoder subtracts it from every later tone (automatic frequency control).

04

A scan line, segment by segment

Every mode sends the picture one line at a time. A line is a fixed sequence: a sync pulse, short porches of black, then the colour channels one after the other. RGB modes send green, blue and red. Robot and PD modes send luminance Y and the colour differences R-Y and B-Y, and save time by sending colour at lower resolution.

Timing of one line

Scottie puts its sync in the middle of the line, before red. Robot 36 sends R-Y on even lines and B-Y on odd lines, and the 4.5 ms separator (1500 or 2300 Hz) says which. PD modes send two picture rows per sync: Y of row one, R-Y and B-Y shared by both rows, then Y of row two.

05

FM demodulation: from audio back to frequency

The receiver must measure the pitch of the audio at every instant. Two methods are common. Counting zero crossings is simple but jumpy. Quadrature demodulation shifts the signal down to 0 Hz, keeps it as a complex number, and reads the frequency from how fast its angle turns.

06

Sync and slant

If the sender's sound card runs 0.2 % fast, every line arrives 0.2 % early. A receiver that only counts time drifts by a pixel or more per line, and the picture leans. A good receiver finds every 1200 Hz sync pulse, fits a straight line through their times, and uses the fitted line period instead.

Same audio, two receivers

Robot 36 at 22 dB SNR. The dots are the sync times the decoder found, measured from where nominal timing expects them. The yellow line is the least-squares fit. Its slope is the clock error, and the receiver reads it back to 0.001 %.

Fitting the line period

For each line k the decoder searches near the predicted time for the falling edge of a 1200 Hz pulse. Syncs more than about 1.5 ms off the fit are left out, so a burst of noise does not bend the line. Then line k starts at a + kP, and its pixels stretch by P/L.

07

Noise, fading and a radio off tune

On a real band the picture fights noise, fades and a receiver that is a little off frequency. Noise makes the frequency estimate jump, and a jump is a wrong pixel: coloured speckle. A fade lowers the signal under the noise for a few seconds and smears a band of lines. A tuning error shifts every tone, so the picture goes light or dark unless the receiver corrects it.

Break the channel

SNR is measured over the whole audio band, 0 to 5.5 kHz. Mistuning shifts every tone by the same amount, as an SSB radio off frequency does. Drift adds a slow shift from the start to the end of the picture, as Doppler does. AFC measures the leader and corrects a fixed error, not a drift. Below about 0 dB the VIS is lost; the decoder then forces the mode, as you can on a real receiver.

How to see a stereogram
  1. Wall-eyed (parallel). Bring the screen close to your nose, so it is a blur. Look through it, as at a far wall. Move back slowly and keep the eyes relaxed. The two guide dots at the top become four, then three: hold the middle one, and the shape comes out of the dots.
  2. Cross-eyed. Hold a finger half way between your eyes and the screen and look at the tip. The two guide dots behind it become three. Take the finger away and keep that crossing.
  3. The two methods show opposite depth. Set the button to the method you use; every stereogram on the page follows it.
  4. Some people cannot see stereo depth. The "Show the depth maps" button shows each hidden shape.

How the stereogram came to be

Each eye sees the world from a point about six centimetres from the other. The brain reads the small differences between the two views as depth. In 1838 Charles Wheatstone showed that two flat drawings, one for each eye, are enough to see a solid. In 1960 Bela Julesz showed that the drawings need no visible shape at all: two fields of random dots can hold one. In 1979 Christopher Tyler and Maureen Clarke put both views into one picture. This page follows that line, with a live stereogram of a real object in every chapter.

01

Two eyes, two views

Look at a point. Both lines of sight meet there. A nearer point needs the eyes to turn in more, a farther point less. The difference between those two angles is the binocular disparity, and the visual brain turns it into depth. Drag the fixation point and the second point in the top view.

Top view of the two eyes

exact geometry

The dashed circle passes through both eyes and the fixation point (the Vieth-Müller circle). Every point on it has zero disparity, because all the angles on one chord of a circle are equal. Points inside it have crossed disparity (they look nearer), points outside it uncrossed (farther). The eye separation is the 6.35 cm (2.5 inches) that Thimbleby, Inglis and Witten use for their stereograms .

02

Wheatstone's mirrors and Brewster's wallpaper

In a paper read to the Royal Society on 21 June 1838, Charles Wheatstone drew each eye's view of a solid as a flat outline and put one drawing before each eye with two mirrors. The drawings joined into one solid figure. Six years later David Brewster found the same depth in a plain repeating wallpaper.

The mirror stereoscope, from above

schematic after the 1838 text

Each eye looks into its own mirror and sees the picture on its side. The reflected images lie where the lines of sight cross, so the eyes converge and focus as on a real object. Wheatstone used only outlines on purpose :

Brewster's lens stereoscope, which he showed to the British Association in Birmingham in 1849, became popular after Jules Duboscq of Paris built it for sale and showed it at the Great Exhibition of 1851 .

03

Julesz: depth with nothing to see

Bela Julesz came to Bell Laboratories from Hungary in 1956 as a radar engineer. On the Bell Labs computers he made pairs of random-dot images: the same dots in both, except that a region is shifted sideways in one of them and the gap it leaves is filled with new dots. Each image alone is noise. Seen with both eyes, the region floats . The 1960 paper showed that the brain can match the two views with no shape or edge visible to either eye . He called this depth "cyclopean" .

Why it mattered

Before Julesz, one could think that each eye first finds the objects and the brain then pairs them. A random-dot stereogram has no objects for one eye to find. The match must happen between dots, before any shape exists: the shape is an output of stereo vision, not an input.

Julesz's 1971 book Foundations of Cyclopean Perception made random-dot stereograms a standard tool . In 1962, with Joan Miller, he coded two independent depth surfaces in one pair . Tyler later noted that one image of such a pair is, in effect, a random-dot autostereogram, but that there is no record that Julesz noticed this .

Some accounts date the first random-dot images to 1959; the paper is from September 1960. date unsure

04

Tyler and Clarke: both views in one image

A random-dot stereogram needs two images and a way to give one to each eye. Brewster's wallpaper needs only one image, but it can show only a few flat planes. In 1979 at the Smith-Kettlewell Eye Research Institute in San Francisco, Christopher Tyler, a former colleague of Julesz at Bell Labs, and the programmer Maureen Clarke joined the two ideas: an algorithm that turns any depth map into one image of random dots that repeats with a spacing set by the depth. They made the first ones on an Apple II and a dot-matrix printer .

the real row of a model

The look-back rule

Start with a strip of random dots one repeat wide. Then, moving right, give each dot the colour of the dot one repeat to its left. Where the depth map says "near", the repeat is shorter; where it says "far", longer. When the eyes fuse neighbouring repeats, each change of spacing is a disparity, so the dots take the shape of the depth map .

Tyler and Clarke also found that when the repeat grows at an edge, the copied dots must be replaced by new ones, or the pattern "rings". Their 1990 SPIE paper gave the algorithm and four other forms: depth that cycles, a wallpaper that tiles a wall, a two-way image with depth at 90°, and the autolustergram .

    05

    Christopher Tyler's research

    The autostereogram is one item in a long research record. Tyler trained in Britain (Ph.D., Keele, 1970), was a research fellow in Julesz's department at Bell Laboratories in 1974-75 and joined the Smith-Kettlewell Eye Research Institute in 1975. His published work runs from stereo vision and flicker to the brain response of infants, symmetry, brain imaging, and the geometry of art . This chapter keeps to that public record.

        06

        From the lab to the poster shop

        In the 1980s random-dot autostereograms appeared in a vision-science text and as the frontispiece of a physics book, and in 1990 in a computer-game magazine . In the early 1990s companies began to sell them in full colour, made from patterns and pictures instead of dots, and a long series of books of them began in 1993. Tyler notes that many of these images ask the eyes to diverge, which most people find harder than crossing them .

          07

          How it works today

          In 1994 Harold Thimbleby, Stuart Inglis and Ian Witten published a symmetric version of the algorithm . Each point of the object links two pixels, one for each eye, placed evenly about it. A point that one eye cannot see makes no link (hidden-surface removal). Pick any example to see it large. To make your own, open the Stereogram Maker.

          The geometry of the 1994 paper

          The screen is at distance D, the far plane (z = 0) at 2D, the near plane (z = 1) μD in front of it. E is the eye separation in pixels. A point at depth z links two pixels s(z) apart.

          The paper uses E = 2.5 inches and μ = 1/3. At the far plane s = E/2: the background repeats every half eye separation.

          Where two links share a pixel, they chain. A chain can tie pixels that no single point links, and then a ghost copy of a surface, an "echo", can appear. Hidden-surface removal leaves fewer links, and the paper shows that this reduces echoes.

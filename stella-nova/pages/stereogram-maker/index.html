Pose

Drag the picture to turn the object. While you drag, the picture shows the depth. The stereogram comes back when you let go.

Short words in heavy letters show best. A line of more than about twelve letters gets thin.

A photo has no true depth. The page uses brightness as height, so a real depth map or an object gives a cleaner result.

Depth

Black and white dots, as in the first random-dot stereograms of the 1960s and 1970s. No pattern gives the eye a false hold, so these are often the easiest to fuse.

About

Each column of the picture links to a column one repeat to the side. Where the hidden surface is near, the two linked columns sit closer, so the eyes must meet in front of the wall. The page gives both columns the same colour, and the pair of eyes reads that as depth.

The constraint method with hidden-surface removal is after Thimbleby, Inglis and Witten (1994). The single-image random-dot stereogram came from the work of Christopher Tyler and Maureen Clarke. The history page tells how it came to be.

How to see it

A practice image: a raised square with a ring.
  1. Bring your face close to the picture, so that it is a blur.
  2. Look through the screen, as if at something far behind it. Do not try to focus.
  3. Move back slowly. Keep the blur. The two dots above the picture become three or four.
  4. When the middle two dots join into one, hold your gaze there. The shape comes up out of the pattern.
  1. Hold a finger between your eyes and the picture.
  2. Look at the tip of the finger. The two dots above the picture double.
  3. Move the finger until the two middle dots join into one.
  4. Keep your eyes there and take the finger away. A cross-eyed picture shows the depth the right way only when you set Cross-eyed in View.

Most people see it in less than a minute. Some take a few days of practice, and a few people do not see depth with two eyes at all. If the shape goes in, not out, you are using the other method: change it in View.

How a string makes a note

Every figure here runs live. The lab above uses the same equations on a grid of a few hundred points, 44,100 times a simulated second.

The wave equation

A small piece of string feels the tension pull on both of its ends. When the string is straight the two pulls cancel. When it is curved they do not, and the net force points toward the inside of the curve. Divide that force by the mass of the piece and you get its acceleration:

Here u is the sideways displacement, T the tension and μ the mass per metre. So the acceleration is the curvature times c². This is what the magma colour in the lab shows: bright where the string bends hard, dark where it is straight. A plucked string is a set of straight lines joined at sharp corners, so all the force sits in those corners.

d'Alembert saw in 1747 that any solution is two shapes moving in opposite directions at speed c:

A pluck starts as two equal half-height copies of the triangle, one running each way. At a fixed end each copy comes back upside down. The figure shows the two copies (blue and orange) and their sum (white). The corners are where the force is.

Standing waves

The waves bounce between the ends, so only shapes that fit a whole number of half wavelengths survive. These are the modes. Mode n has n loops, n − 1 still points inside (nodes) and moves as a whole at one frequency:

Any motion of the string is a sum of these modes. The bars under the lab string show how much of each mode is on the string now.

This is Mersenne's law (1636). Halve the length and the pitch goes up an octave: that is the 12th fret. Four times the tension also gives an octave. A heavier string (larger μ) is lower, which is why the bass strings are wound with wire: more mass without more stiffness.

Why the pluck position changes the tone

A pluck at fraction p of the length gives mode n the amplitude

A mode with a node at the pluck point gets nothing. Pluck at the middle (p = 1/2) and every even harmonic is gone: the tone is round and hollow. Pluck near the bridge and many high modes stay: the tone is bright and thin. Guitarists play near the sound hole, about p = 1/5, so the 5th, 10th and 15th harmonics are missing.

Inharmonicity: real strings are stiff

A steel string also resists bending. That adds a fourth-derivative term, and high modes go a little sharp:

For guitar strings B is about 10⁻⁵ to 10⁻⁴, so the 8th harmonic is a few cents sharp. A piano's short bass strings are much worse, which is why tuners stretch the octaves. Turn up Stiffness in the lab and watch the spectrum lines move right of the grid.

Frets and twelve equal steps

Each fret shortens the string by the same factor, 2−1/12, so each step is the same ratio, 100 cents:

This makes every key sound the same, but only the octave is a pure ratio. The equal fifth (27/12 = 1.4983) is 2 cents flat of 3/2. The equal major third (1.2599) is 14 cents sharp of 5/4, and you can hear it as a slow beat.

How the body makes it loud

A string is too thin to push much air. It pushes the bridge instead, with the force

The bridge drives the top plate, and the top and the air inside the box have resonances of their own. Near a resonance the body radiates much more. The guitar's air mode near 100 Hz gives the bass its boom. A violin's main body modes sit near 280, 470 and 550 Hz, and its bridge adds a broad lift near 2.5 kHz.

Bowing: stick and slip

A bow does not pluck. Rosin makes the hair grip the string, which moves with the bow (stick) until the string's pull is larger than friction. Then it snaps back (slip), and grips again. Helmholtz found in the 1860s that the string takes a simple shape: two straight lines joined at one sharp corner. The corner runs around a lens-shaped path once per period, and each time it passes the bow, the string slips.

The mathematics of a string

8. The acceleration field: what the colours show

The right side of the equation of motion is the total force per unit mass on each point of the string. That is the acceleration the lab colours:

The tension term is by far the largest, so a ≈ c²u_xx: the colour is the curvature times c². A plucked string is straight lines and corners. The straight parts are dark, because nothing pulls them, and the corners glow, because all the force is there. Watch the corners run to the ends and back: that is d'Alembert's two waves. 9. Impedance and the bridge

A string resists being moved sideways with its wave impedance, the force needed per unit of transverse velocity:

When a wave on string 1 meets a second medium with impedance Z₂, part reflects and part goes on. Matching the force and the velocity at the joint gives:

A guitar bridge has an impedance of order 10²–10³ kg/s; a string about 1 kg/s. So the displacement wave reflects almost fully and upside down (r_u ≈ −1), and only a few parts in a thousand go into the top plate per bounce. That small leak is all the sound you hear from the body, and it is why the note can sustain for seconds. The amplitude decay from the bridge alone is σ = f₁ (−ln|r|).

10. Helmholtz motion of a bowed string

A bow does not pluck once; it sticks and slips every period. Helmholtz saw in 1863 that the result is a single corner running round the string along two parabolas. In modes, every harmonic is present with amplitude 1/n² and the same phase:

The sum is two straight lines meeting at a corner at x = c t (and back). Under the bow the string velocity has only two values: it moves with the bow (stick), then flies back (slip) when the corner passes. The spectrum, 1/n² in displacement and 1/n in velocity, is the bright sawtooth sound of a violin.

11. Beats and coupled strings

Two tones close in frequency add to a tone at the mean frequency whose loudness swells and fades:

So you hear Δf beats per second; tuning two strings means slowing the beats to zero. Strings on the same bridge are also coupled: the bridge moves a little and drives the other strings. Two equal strings with coupling k have two normal modes, and energy swaps between the strings at their difference frequency. That is sympathetic resonance: an untouched string sings when its partner is played.

12. Equal temperament against just ratios

The harmonic series gives the pure intervals: 2:1, 3:2, 5:4 and so on. Frets divide the octave into twelve equal steps instead, so every key works the same. The size of an interval in cents is:

The equal fifth is 2 cents flat of 3:2, almost pure. The equal major third is 14 cents sharp of 5:4, so a fretted major chord always beats a little. When the upper note of an interval p:q has its qth harmonic near the pth harmonic of the lower note, they beat at |q f_upper − p f_lower|.

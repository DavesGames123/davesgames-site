The source

A return stroke heats the channel to about 30 000 K in a few microseconds. The pressure in it rises to many atmospheres and the channel expands as a cylindrical shock. Few (1969) uses the energy per unit length El: the shock becomes a weak acoustic wave near the radius where the work against the air equals that energy.

With El = 5 × 10⁵ J/m, R0 ≈ 2.2 m and the spectrum has its peak fm near 100 Hz. Each short piece of the channel sends an N-wave of length about R0/c.

Why thunder rumbles

The whole channel flashes within about 50 μs, but its pieces are at different distances r from you. The sound of each piece arrives at r/c. The thunder lasts from the nearest point to the farthest point.

A straight piece of channel seen from the side has all its points at nearly the same distance: their N-waves arrive together and add up to a clap. Seen end-on, the same piece spreads its sound over Δt = L/c: a low rumble. The tortuosity σ of the channel sets how many claps you hear. No extra directivity is put in; it comes from the sum.

Air absorption

Air takes energy from sound through viscosity and through the relaxation of oxygen and nitrogen molecules, and water vapour sets the relaxation rates. The page uses the ISO 9613-1 coefficient α(f, T, RH). At 20 °C and 50 % RH it is about 5 dB/km at 1 kHz but only about 0.3 dB/km at 100 Hz. So near thunder cracks, and far thunder only rumbles.

The wavefield in panel (a)

Panel (a) solves the linear acoustic equations on a grid across the plane. The rigid ground and buildings reflect; the edges absorb. The sound speed changes with height:

In a normal atmosphere T falls with height, so c falls too and the wavefronts tilt: rays bend up, away from the ground. Beyond some range no ray reaches the ground, and you do not hear thunder there: the acoustic shadow. A night inversion bends rays down and carries thunder farther. Watch the front near the ground in the energy view.

The grid is a low-pass picture of the wave. It carries only frequencies below about c/(6Δx), a few hertz, so it cannot make the sound. It is 2D: the channel acts as a sheet across the plane, which spreads as 1/√r, like a long channel seen from nearer than its length.

Slopes and walls that can see both the channel and the microphone scatter late echoes (first order, diffuse). Valleys ring; open plains do not.

Limits

Rays in (b) are straight: refraction moves the arrival times a little and the shadow zone is in (a), not in (b). The level is calibrated to one assumption: a straight 2 km channel seen side-on from 1 km gives 5 Pa peak (108 dB) at 5 × 10⁵ J/m. Wind, turbulence, the shock region near the channel and channels out of the plane are not modelled.

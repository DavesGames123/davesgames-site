View

Beads: one sphere per residue. Space-filling: larger beads with buried ones shaded, for a surface look. Tube: a smooth line along each chain, wider on helices and strands. Blobs: each chain as a few smooth bodies, so the subunits read as tiles. Cage: the symmetry lattice, subunit centres joined to their neighbours, and the icosahedron on capsids. Glow: soft points. Toon: flat shades with ink outlines. A change of view is a change of drawing style, not a change in the virus. Key V.

About

A virus is a genome in a protein shell. A small genome codes for few proteins, so the shell is made of many copies of one or a few proteins. The most economical closed shell is an icosahedron: 60 identical copies, related by 5-fold, 3-fold and 2-fold rotation axes. Larger shells put 60 T subunits on a lattice where each copy has almost the same contacts (quasi-equivalence, Caspar and Klug 1962). T = h² + hk + k² takes the values 1, 3, 4, 7, 9, 12, 13 …

Other viruses are helical (tobacco mosaic virus, a rod of 2130 copies), or build a cone (the HIV capsid, closed by 12 pentamers), or wrap their capsid in a lipid membrane studded with spike proteins (coronaviruses, influenza, HIV, Ebola, rabies, measles). The spikes bind receptors on cells and fuse the membranes, and most vaccines and neutralising antibodies target them.

Prions are not viruses: they are proteins with no genome. The prion protein PrP folds into a small helical domain in healthy cells. In prion diseases (Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease, kuru, fatal familial insomnia; scrapie, BSE and chronic wasting disease in animals) it refolds into flat layers that stack into cross-β fibrils, and the fibril ends recruit and convert more PrP. Tau and α-synuclein form similar fibrils in Alzheimer's and Parkinson's disease, but these diseases do not spread between people in daily life.

Sizes for scale: a C–C bond is 0.15 nm, a folded protein 3–10 nm, a small capsid 30 nm, an enveloped virus about 100 nm, a bacterium about 2 µm, a red blood cell 7.5 µm.

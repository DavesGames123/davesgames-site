The product of the ratios from the centre wheel to the fourth wheel is 80/10 × 75/10 = 60: one turn per hour becomes one turn per minute.

How it works

Power. A coiled steel mainspring sits in the barrel. The crown winds it through the crown wheel and the ratchet; the click stops it from unwinding back. The spring then turns the barrel, slowly: once in 8 hours.

The going train. The barrel drives the centre wheel, which carries the minute hand and turns once an hour. Each stage steps up the speed: the third wheel, then the fourth wheel, which carries the small seconds hand and turns once a minute.

The escapement. Left alone, the train would spin out in seconds. The escape wheel is held by two ruby pallet stones on the lever. Each swing of the balance kicks the lever across: one stone lets a tooth go, the tooth slides over the stone and pushes the lever (the impulse that keeps the balance going), and the other stone catches the next tooth. That is the tick.

The balance. The balance wheel and its hairspring form an oscillator. This one runs at 2.5 Hz: 5 beats a second, 18,000 an hour. Two beats let one escape tooth go, and the escape wheel turns once in 6 seconds.

The hands. Under the dial, the cannon pinion on the centre arbor drives the minute wheel, and its pinion drives the hour wheel: 36/12 × 40/10 = 12, so the hour hand turns once in 12 hours.

The calibre is an original design. Its tooth counts and modules follow the class of the Unitas/ETA 6497 pocket-watch calibre (36.6 mm, 18,000 vph). The tooth outlines are cycloidal-style horological profiles, and a test checks every mesh for overlap. The pallet stones and escape teeth come from a collision solve, so the escape wheel turns only as far as the stones allow.
